The short answer: not today, and probably not this month — but yes, eventually, and the timing usually isn't yours to pick.

That's the part homeowners don't expect. Most people assume they'll replace Poly-B when it starts leaking. In practice, it's an insurance renewal letter or a buyer's home inspection that forces the decision, and both tend to arrive on someone else's schedule. Knowing which of those is coming for you first is most of the answer.

Here's how we think about it when we walk a Calgary home.

## First, confirm it's actually Poly-B

Plenty of people are told they have Poly-B when they don't, and vice versa.

Poly-B is flexible plastic tubing, usually grey, sometimes white or cream. Look for **PB2110** stamped along the length of the pipe — that stamping is the giveaway, and it's printed repeatedly so you don't need a long exposed run to find it.

Check three places: under the kitchen and bathroom sinks, around the hot water tank, and along the ceiling of an unfinished basement or mechanical room. If the home was built between roughly 1978 and 1995, there's a real chance it's in there somewhere.

One thing that trips people up: a previous owner may have replaced the visible sections and left everything inside the walls. Grey pipe under the sink means Poly-B. Copper or PEX under the sink means very little on its own.

## What actually fails, and why nobody can date it for you

The pipe itself is only half the story. In most of the failures we're called out to, the failure point is a **fitting**, not a straight run of tubing.

The original Poly-B systems used plastic acetal insert fittings held with metal crimp rings. Those fittings sit at every joint, elbow and tee — including the ones buried behind drywall — and they become brittle with age. Chlorine in municipal water oxidizes the material from the inside, which is why the interior surface degrades where you can't see it while the outside of the pipe still looks fine.

That's the honest, frustrating part of this: **there is no inspection that tells you how much life is left.** You can't scope it, you can't pressure-test a reliable answer out of it, and two identical homes on the same street can be twenty years apart in when they fail. Anyone who tells you your Poly-B has five good years left is guessing.

So the decision can't be made on pipe condition. It has to be made on your exposure.

## The three things that actually set your timeline

**Insurance.** This is the one that catches most Calgary homeowners. Carriers have tightened steadily on Poly-B — some surcharge it, some restrict water-damage coverage, some decline to renew until it's replaced, and some won't write a new policy on the home at all. If you're happy with your current policy and your renewal is quiet, you have time. If you're shopping insurance, switching carriers, or you've just had a renewal letter asking about your plumbing, your timeline just got a lot shorter. Call your broker and ask the direct question: *what is your position on polybutylene, and does it change my coverage?*

**Resale.** If you're selling within a couple of years, this shows up on the inspection report and becomes a negotiation. You'll typically either replace it or discount for it, and replacing it on your own schedule with a written scope tends to go better than conceding a number under offer pressure.

**What's already happened in this house.** One failed fitting is the most useful data point you'll get. The rest of the system is the same age, the same material, and has seen the same water. When we see a home that's had a fitting let go, we stop treating replacement as a someday item.

## Replace now if any of these are true

- **A fitting or pipe has already failed**, even once, even if the repair was small.
- **Your insurer has asked about it**, restricted coverage, or set a condition on renewal.
- **You're listing within about two years.**
- **The system has visible trouble** — kinked or crimped runs, discolouration, weeping at fittings, or a section that was pinched during a past renovation.
- **You're already opening walls.** If a basement development or a bathroom renovation is happening anyway, the drywall cost is already paid. This is the cheapest Poly-B replacement you will ever be offered, and it's the one people most often regret skipping.

## You can reasonably wait if

- The system has **never leaked**, has no visible damage, and
- Your **insurance is stable** and you've confirmed your carrier's position, and
- You have **no plans to sell soon**, and
- The home has **no unusual pressure or temperature issues** driving extra stress on the system.

Waiting is a legitimate choice. It's not the choice we'd make with a 40-year-old system, but it's defensible if all four hold. What makes it a bad choice is waiting *by default* — never asking the insurance question, never budgeting for it, and then handling it as an emergency with wet carpet and a restoration crew in the house.

If you are going to wait, do two things: get the insurance answer in writing, and know where your main shutoff is and that it actually turns. If a fitting does go, the minutes between the failure and the water stopping decide how expensive the day becomes. ## What replacement actually involves

Full replacement means running new supply lines through the house and abandoning the Poly-B in place. Most Calgary homes go to PEX — it's flexible enough to pull through existing wall cavities with far less demolition than copper, it handles our temperature swings well, and it carries a real manufacturer warranty. Copper still makes sense for exposed runs and a few specific situations, and we'll say so when it does.

The work you feel most is the drywall, not the plumbing. A good crew plans access points before cutting anything, and the difference between a tidy job and a mess is almost entirely in that planning.

On partial replacement: it's better than nothing, but be clear about what you're buying. Doing the visible basement and mechanical-room runs while leaving the in-wall lines means you've replaced the pipe you could see and left the fittings you couldn't. If budget means staging the work, stage it toward the areas where a failure would do the most damage — above finished space, not below it.

A note worth adding, since it comes up on nearly every one of these jobs: Calgary's hard water doesn't cause Poly-B failure, but it's hard on whatever you install next, and on your water heater and boiler at the same time. If you're already spending on a repipe, it's a sensible moment to ask whether a [water softener](/water-softener/) belongs in the plan.
